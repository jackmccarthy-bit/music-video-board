Related examples

Two videos by Michel Gondry that take the “visuals that move with the music” idea furthest. The LCD Soundsystem video on my board is a homage to them.

Two patterns across my picks

Looking at my picks together, two ideas keep coming back. Each one suggests a different kind of video I could make, and a different set of skills to practice.

1. Ordinary places turned into dance floors

The Weekend It’s Oh So Quiet When Love Takes Over

All three start somewhere everyone recognizes and nobody would call glamorous: an office, an auto shop and the street outside, the Venice Beach boardwalk. Then the music takes over the space. In “The Weekend” one office worker climbs onto the photocopier and pulls her coworkers in. In “It’s Oh So Quiet” Björk wanders a drab street until the chorus hits and the whole block becomes a Hollywood musical. In “When Love Takes Over” street performers keep joining until the walk becomes a night party on the beach.

The joke and the joy come from the contrast. The place stays ordinary, so the dancing feels like it’s breaking a rule. The crowd also grows over the length of the song, so the video has a shape even without much story.

Start small, end bigOne person starts and the crowd grows. The build gives the video its arc.
Let the song decide the turnsThe big shifts land on the song’s structure: the countdown to Saturday, the jump from whispered verse to loud chorus.
Keep the place realReal props (a photocopier, a garage, a boardwalk) make the shift into dancing land harder.

What it would take: one location I can get into, a few friends willing to dance, simple choreography non-dancers can learn, and a plan that marks on the song’s timeline when each new person joins.

2. Visuals that move with the music

Ray of Light Daft Punk Is Playing at My House Around the World Star Guitar

These videos make the music visible. Instead of telling a story, they give each sound something to look at. “Ray of Light” speeds the whole world up with time-lapse until the cities rush at the song’s pace. “Daft Punk Is Playing at My House” lines up performers in colored spandex so they read like a graphic equalizer. That video is itself a homage to the two related examples, both directed by Michel Gondry, who takes the idea furthest.

Gondry used two methods. In “Around the World,” each group of dancers plays one instrument: robots for the vocals, athletes for the bass, swimmers for the keyboard, skeletons for the guitar, mummies for the drum machine. Watching the dancers is like reading the song. In “Star Guitar,” the landscape outside a train window does the same job, with buildings and poles passing on the beats. He plotted the song on graph paper before he made it.

Map sounds to peopleGive each instrument a costume or a move (“Around the World,” the LCD video).
Map sounds to thingsMake objects, cuts or the scenery hit the beats (“Star Guitar”).
Change time itselfSpeed up or slow down the footage so the world matches the tempo (“Ray of Light”).

What it would take: listening closely enough to chart every part of a song, then shooting or editing to that chart. A good small exercise: pick 15 seconds of a song, write out each sound, and assign it an object or a move.

What the board has in common

Beyond the two patterns, here is what links all nine videos on the board.

  • One clear ideaEach video can be described in a sentence: an office party, a musical in a garage, a city in time-lapse, a stage of robots and mummies. The idea carries the video more than the budget does.
  • The song’s structure drives the pictureThe quiet and loud sections in “It’s Oh So Quiet,” the countdown in “The Weekend,” the separate instrument parts in “Around the World.” The video is built from the song, not laid over it.
  • Borrowing on purpose“It’s Oh So Quiet” borrows from Hollywood musicals, “Ray of Light” from the film Koyaanisqatsi, and “Daft Punk Is Playing at My House” from Gondry and Spike Jonze. Remixing a known form is the same move as this week’s meme remixes.
  • Bodies more than effectsMost of these run on dancers, performers and real places rather than digital effects. The exception is “claws,” where a green screen does the work, and even that was shot at home. Several could be made on a small budget with good planning.
  • Shared peopleJonas Åkerlund directed two of my picks (“Ray of Light,” “When Love Takes Over”). Daft Punk connect three videos: their own two, and the LCD Soundsystem song named after them. Michel Gondry directed both examples, and the LCD video is a homage to him.
  • Dance and electronic musicEight of the nine are dance, electronic or pop tracks with a strong beat. The exception is “It’s Oh So Quiet,” a big-band cover of a 1951 song, and even that one runs on sudden shifts in volume.

Next directions

What the board is pointing me toward, starting from my own notes and building them into a plan for the Unit 2 project.

My notes

  • I like the idea of combining a music video filmed on a single set or in a single place with time-lapse footage and surrealist visuals.
  • “claws” by Charli XCX maybe starts to combine those ideas.
  • Could it work for an a cappella music video with multiple people?
  • I like the color scheme of “Lose Yourself to Dance.”
  • I would do less dance than “The Weekend,” but I like that the speeds change.

The idea in one sentence

A group sings a cappella in one room. The singers stay in real time while the room around them speeds up, changes color and turns surreal.

Building it out

One setOne room keeps the whole group in frame and gives me control over light and props. “The Weekend” and “It’s Oh So Quiet” transform one ordinary place, and “claws” never leaves home. Locking the camera in place lets every shot of the room line up.
Time-lapseLike “Ray of Light,” the room can rush through a day while the song plays. Its math is a useful reality check: one frame every ten seconds means half an hour of shooting for five seconds on screen. Light moving across the walls from morning to night could be the arc of the video.
Changing speedsWhat I like in “The Weekend” is the shifts in speed, not the dancing. The speed could follow the song’s sections: real time for the verses, time-lapse for the choruses, a sudden slowdown on the quietest moment.
Surreal visuals“claws” gets its surreal look from a green screen on a small budget. Practical tricks work too: objects that appear and vanish between time-lapse frames, impossible backgrounds in the window, the room changing color mid-phrase.
A cappella, many peopleWith no instruments, the voices are the band. Taking a cue from “Around the World,” each voice part (bass, beatbox, harmonies, lead) could get its own person, place in the room and color, so you can see who is making each sound.
ColorBuild a palette from stills of “Lose Yourself to Dance” and match it with lights and clothing. Each voice part could get one color from that palette.
Less dance, more movementInstead of choreography, the movement comes from the camera, the speed changes and simple staging: singers turning, stepping forward for their part, or freezing while the room moves around them.

Small studies to try

  • Time-lapse testShoot 30 minutes of one room with a locked phone and see what five seconds of it looks like.
  • Voice mapTake 15 seconds of an a cappella song, list every voice part, and give each one a person, a spot and a color.
  • Speed switchEdit one clip that moves from real time to time-lapse and back on the beat.
  • Surreal shotTry one green-screen or in-camera trick, like an object that appears between frames.
  • Color testLight one person with the palette and compare it to the reference stills.

Questions to answer next

  • Which song? An existing a cappella arrangement, or one we arrange ourselves?
  • How many singers, and who? If I can’t find enough, I could sing several parts myself and appear several times in the frame.
  • Record the audio first and lip-sync, or record live on set? Time-lapse is easier with a finished track.
  • Which room, and can I leave the camera and lights set up for a whole day?
  • How do the singers stay in real time while the room is in time-lapse? Green screen, shooting them separately and layering, or a simpler compromise?
